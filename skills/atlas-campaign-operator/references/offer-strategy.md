## Source and claim authority

1. Apply current explicit user instructions and active confirmation rules.
2. Use current, approved same-client facts for price, scope, capability, terms, proof, capacity and assets; use the actual thread for prior promises.
3. Use the user's Drive SOPs as working methods, reconciling contradictions rather than pretending they agree.
4. Use verified primary creator videos/posts as conditional ideas. Keep creator claims, user-SOP examples and our adaptations distinct.
5. Label unverified hypotheses. Unknown is a valid answer; an unknown must not become a campaign claim.

Source visibility or an old client draft is not commercial approval. Never reuse another client's results, certification, price, calendar or fulfillment promise. Never imply a named company endorses this client because it demonstrates a similar mechanism.

## Build the offer

### 1. Establish one buyer and a real reason to care

Retrieve the client's service/onboarding, current offer sheet, relevant wins/losses, buyer questions, delivery limits, current campaign evidence and Atlas's thesis. Do not ask for facts already available.

Define the buyer's role, company/use case, current alternative, one problem or desired improvement, and why solving it matters. A healthy business can want an improvement; do not manufacture pain. Use the buyer's operational language: yield, lead time, quality, capacity, RFQ fit, time, cost, risk, revenue or status as appropriate. Do not force every manufacturer into a lead-generation offer.

Ground relevance in either a coherent verified segment or a current investment/event signal. For each signal, distinguish what was observed from the hypothesis it supports. Hiring does not prove pipeline failure; ads do not prove wasted spend; absent website wording does not prove absent capability. Without a verified signal, use honest segment relevance.

### 2. Extract the client-specific advantage

Use source answers where available; ask only the most consequential unresolved question:
- What is the buyer already investing in, and what outcome is that meant to produce?
- What breaks or remains difficult with the current alternative?
- What does this client actually do: inputs → process → deliverable?
- What relevant evidence makes that credible, and what does it not prove?
- What useful first step can the client reliably provide, at what cost and capacity?
- What limits, conditions and exclusions must the buyer know?

Reject invented branded mechanisms. An ordinary process with credible proof is preferable to fake uniqueness.

### 3. Separate the paid outcome from the entry offer

Write the paid outcome and the immediate deliverable separately. An audit, sample, list, feasibility review or meeting is not the eventual business result. A desirable result without a credible path is not a strong offer.

Choose the smallest useful next step from the buyer's state and client capability:
- Tangible service preview when valuable, relevant, repeatable and economically feasible.
- Specific insight, benchmark or example when it genuinely answers a buyer need.
- Scoped paid pilot/starter when free work would be costly or misleading.
- Direct product, RFQ, technical discussion or meeting when genuine buying context makes it appropriate.
- Research/interview only when that project genuinely exists and any commercial transition is transparent.

Do not require free work, a guarantee, a named mechanism or a call for every offer. Do not dismiss a catalog, technical sheet or credible insight because a generic lead-magnet hierarchy ranks it lower. Recipient usefulness and fulfillment determine the choice.

### 4. Make the value believable

Prefer relevant same-client evidence over prestigious but unrelated logos. One strong proof element can be enough; a second should add information rather than inflate the message. Category evidence supports a mechanism, not this client's performance. Quantify only when the source, scope, timeframe and approval support the precise wording.

Connect economic logic to verified inputs or label a scenario. Do not turn assumed conversion rates, prospect revenue or a creator's success story into facts. Consider effort, switching cost, implementation burden and time-to-value, including burdens that remain.

### 5. Choose risk reduction deliberately

Default to clear scope, a useful first step, transparent inputs and believable evidence. Use a guarantee only when approved, exact, controllable enough, financially survivable and relevant. No automatic guarantee-first or guarantee-ban rule. Route new material commercial terms for approval; route legal exposure to Jarvis legal/HR module.

Do not exploit sunk-cost thinking, invent scarcity, imply a diagnosis that was not done, or add effort merely to make the buyer more invested. Ask for inputs or a call only when they have a genuine purpose and the condition is disclosed before agreement.

### 6. Pass the hard gate, then recommend

Complete the recipe and readiness checks in the reference. A creative score cannot override missing material facts, unsupported claims, unapproved terms or missing fulfillment capacity. Mark `concept`, `needs_client_facts`, `ready_for_review` or `approved_for_test` with approval evidence. Atlas offer strategy's recommendation is never approval by itself.

Compare up to three meaningfully different viable angles. If only one is supported, provide one plus the exact dependencies for alternatives. Recommend a controlled test; do not promise a lift.

## Promise fidelity

Record what, quantity, mode, inputs, free/paid status, disclosed conditions, owner, approved turnaround, capacity and evidence of actual fulfillment. A clearly agreed call-delivered offer is valid; deliver the agreed usable value without a surprise purchase. An offer to email an asset cannot acquire a hidden call gate later. Booking or a screen-share alone is not delivery of a promised take-away asset.

Preserve older promises when context changes. Keep the canonical Scribe contract authoritative. Do not change Scribe reply decisions behavior, live campaigns, replier settings or sending controls through an offer review.

## Diagnose before replacing

For an existing offer, ask Atlas for delivery/list validity, volume and observation window, relevant reply categories, fulfillment, qualified progression and sales outcomes. Distinguish low reach, irrelevant recipients, weak belief, asset-only interest, fulfillment failure, ownership delay and sales-stage loss. Raw reply count does not isolate offer strength.

Check the buyer-facing path before blaming the offer: compare the current website or landing page, relevant founder/company profiles and linked proof with the approved offer. Flag contradictions, unclear deliverables and important unanswered buyer questions for the owner. This is a consistency check, not a requirement to build a VSL or redesign a site. A conversational opener that creates more dialogue also needs adequate reply ownership and fulfillment capacity; include that work in the test.

Preserve a winning concept unless a specific failure justifies change. Test one material lever at a time. Small-TAM and technical/procurement motions may need account-level relevance and RFQ/sample progression rather than volume or meeting targets.

## Return an actionable offer sheet

Keep the recommendation first:
1. Decision and status, with the main reason.
2. One-sentence offer recipe plus immediate next step.
3. Buyer/problem, relevance evidence, mechanism, proof and limitations.
4. Exact promise/fulfillment fields and commercial approval gaps.
5. Up to three distinct angles, recommended test and downstream success measure.
6. Claims allowed / claims prohibited, sources and a compact Scribe/Atlas handoff.

When revising, show the material before/after and why it changed. Do not bury a useful draft beneath a long workshop. Finish once it passes the gate; do not polish indefinitely.
