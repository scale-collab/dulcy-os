## 1. Hard copy rules

Each rule: exact wording where possible, then the section it comes from. "Strength" = how firmly the doc states it.

### 1.1 Length
- "Rule 2: Shorter Is Better. In roughly 90% of cases, if you can say something in fewer words, you should." / "The rule: say the same thing in fewer words. You almost always can." (SOP: Cold Email Copywriting Framework, Matt/Anivvo)
- "This email is 4 sentences. It can be made 3. It can be made 2. Shorter is always an option." (same, Example 2)
- "the email template is four lines. hey [name]. opener. irresistible offer. cta. that's the whole thing." (7 principles post, principle 3)
- "Keep the email short without cutting material terms." (Signal framework, Dulcy-adapted). Short never overrides honesty: conditions, costs and inputs stay in.

### 1.2 Structure (every email must have all parts)
- "Every good cold email contains all three of these parts. They can be written as separate lines, combined into one line, or structured however makes sense — but all three elements must be present." Part 1 "Why Are You Reaching Out to Me Specifically?", Part 2 "What Are You Selling Me and Why Should I Trust You?", Part 3 "What Do You Want Me to Do Next?" (The 3-Part Cold Email Framework, Matt)
- "Answer this as fast as possible. This is your call out and/or personalization." (Part 1)
- "After the call out, go straight into the pitch. Do not bury it. Do not delay. If someone never gets to the pitch, the pitch can never land." (Part 2)
- "Do not re-explain the problem to someone who already lives it ... Skip the 'most physicians struggle with X' framing — that is ad copy. Go straight to your solution." (Part 2 nuance)
- 4-part version adds: "Part 4 — Reread as the Recipient ... Identify every objection you would have ... Address those objections proactively inside the email." (SOP: The Full Cold Email System, Matt Lucero)
- Solve one problem: "Solving more than one problem in a single piece of outreach — even if your full service solves 50 things, your offer should name one" (Dulcy guide, Part 6 common mistakes)

### 1.3 Tone and style
- "Rule 1: Write in a 1-to-1 Style, Not Ad Copy Style." Ad copy = "Introducing this product…", "A lot of marketers struggle with this...", "Talking about an amorphous third party", "Grandiose, commercial tone". 1-to-1 = "The word 'you' used frequently". Frame: "Imagine you are knocking on their door or meeting them at an event." (Matt)
- "Rule 4: Act Human ... sound almost like a text message." Avoid "Corporate jargon", "Trying to sound like a literary masterpiece", "Overly formal sentence structures", "Perfectly eloquent paragraphs". Use "Abbreviations where appropriate", "Informal language and sentence structure (not sloppy — just natural)". "The level of informality should match the market." (Matt)
- "Clear beats clever. (Credit: Alex Hormozi.) ... Say exactly what your product does in plain language." (Copy Principles, Matt Lucero)
- "Write like a friend texting a coworker. If your email sounds corporate, formal, or like a cold email template, it gets deleted like a cold email." (same)
- "just write like a real person with a real reason to reach out." (7 principles)
- "make the offer stupidly clear ... if a prospect can't understand what you're offering and why they should care in 5 seconds flat, you've lost them" (7 principles)
- Niche language: "Normally, simplicity is preferred. The exception is when niche-specific language increases trust ... Do not use insider language merely to sound clever." (SOP Building a Cold-Ready Offer, Step 5)
- Worldview alignment: make the prospect feel "They understand my world." Weak: "We help businesses like yours grow." (same, Step 3)
- Audience etiquette: "When writing to doctors, 'Hi [First Name]' may offend — use 'Dr. [Last Name]' instead." (Additional Notes on Copywriting Process)
- Hormozi benefits: "Every benefit should answer: who will envy them for this?" (Hormozi post)
- "Best language for value props comes from your sales process. The actual words your leads use on sales calls and in replies are the best copy you will ever write." (Additional Notes)
- Be specific about pain: "Sharp, specific pain points only the right person recognizes get the reply" (Dulcy guide, Part 4 #9); "Resist the urge to write a pain point broad enough to apply to 'everyone in the industry.'" (Fazio #12)
- Believability: "Do not maximize only the dream outcome. A bigger promise can actually lower trust." / "Every large reduction in time or effort must have a mechanism." / "Every bold claim needs a reason it is possible." (Cold-Ready Offer SOP, Steps 2, 6, 7)
- Lead with math: "Lead with the inevitable math, not a hopeful outcome." (Fazio #2)

### 1.4 Banned / avoid words and phrases (from the doc)
- **Spam words:** "Be way more granular with specific words and phrases in your copy." / "We had a client with a TRUE no-brainer offer get 0 replies after 24h. I replaced the word 'free' with complimentary in the script. The very next email generated a lead. Spam words are no joke." (Alex's top notes, lines 14-18). No full spam-word list exists in the doc: **UNKNOWN**; use EmailGuard (RULES 2026-11-17).
- Ad-copy openers: "Introducing this product…", "A lot of marketers struggle with this. We built a solution to help marketers…", "most physicians struggle with X".
- Vague claims: "we help businesses grow" ("invisible in an inbox"), "We help businesses like yours grow", "An innovative solution that helps you with your staffing and recruiting needs and hiring candidates that were never imaginable." (bad example)
- Empty guarantees: "Customer satisfaction guaranteed", "100% satisfaction guaranteed", "Money back if you aren't happy", "Dedicated account management team", "Based in America", "I'll work forever until it works" (tier list).
- Weak offers named as CTA bait: "free audit", "free consultation", "pick my brain", generic "strategy call", "webinar invite", "launch discount".
- Hype: "We'll 10X your revenue.", "We'll 10X your pipeline with zero effort.", "We'll make you go viral on LinkedIn."
- Company-bio pitch: "We're honest. 80% renewal rate. Five years in business." → "That's not an offer. That's a company bio." (MCA dialogue)

### 1.5 Proof
- "On proof and social proof: This is the single heaviest lifting element of the value prop. Do not talk about features and bells and whistles. Instead, give them a reason to believe you." Types: number of clients/users, volume, results, industry-specific ("even stronger because it's relevant to them"), named endorsements, specific stats. "The more specific and relevant the proof is to their world, the more convincing it is." (Matt, Part 2)
- Recognisable names beat big numbers: "No number at all, but everyone knows Apple, so the proof is much more effective." (150M-emails post, [3])
- Breadth over depth: "One operator tripled their reply rate — from 1% to 3% — just by changing their P.S. line from one detailed case study to a quick bulleted list of about 30 recognizable client names." (Dulcy guide #10; Fazio #13: "default to breadth ... unless the single story includes a stat too specific to ignore.")
- Counterpoint at the end of the doc (transcript): a "very bland case study" kills momentum; "Be very specific" with numbers, timeline and outcome (landscaping story, section 4).
- Checklist: "Proof: 1-2 tight proof points (numbers or examples)" (Offer Packaging Checklist).
- **Dulcy proof rule (Signal framework):** "use one approved result from this sending client's work with a genuinely comparable customer. Preserve the exact metric, timeframe, attribution and limitations. Record the source and approval. If relevant verified proof is missing, omit the result sentence or use an honest mechanism/observation; never invent a case study to complete the formula."
- RULES wins: at least 1 strong proof per email; extra proof only if equally strong (RULES 2026-11-25). Proof is client-specific: never reuse one client's facts for another (CLAUDE.md #3).

### 1.6 CTA
- "End every cold email with a clear, specific call to action. Do not pitch someone and then leave them with nothing." (Matt, Part 3)
- "Make the CTA valuable ... if you can offer something for free, that is always stronger than just asking for their time." (Matt)
- When to use which: "Direct meeting ask: works best if you have a strong, established, easy-to-digest offer with clear proof" / "If your product is complicated ... a free asset or explainer as the CTA is a lower-friction bridge." (Matt)
- "Four CTAs that convert in 2026: 30-minute call — only use this if you have a blue ocean product/service ... For most people, this is too aggressive for cold traffic. Soft ask — 'Can I send you more info?' ... Free portfolio or sample ... Free deliverable — ... Highest converting CTA when applicable." (Matt Lucero)
- "Make the CTA a non-decision. 'Want me to set it up? Takes 30 seconds and it's free.' Not asking for a demo or sending a calendar link." (Nick, Slack ad-bot thread)
- "CTA: one simple next step (reply 'yes' / send a link / ask 2 questions)" (Offer Packaging Checklist)
- **Dulcy Signal framework:** "Low-friction CTA: ask for one specific next step, such as permission to send the named creative brief. Make clear what they receive and any material cost, input, call or eligibility condition. A yes to 'send it' is permission to receive that asset, not evidence of purchase intent." / "Never bait-and-switch an emailed asset into a mandatory call or purchase."
- Guarantee placement: "A guarantee belongs in the CTA layer of cold outreach ... not buried in the proof section. Its job is reply generation." (Fazio #11) Only with an approved guarantee.
- RULES wins: default ask = low friction, one-word reply; call ask only if it is the only logical next step (RULES 2026-11-25).

### 1.7 Subject lines
- "Subject lines: Write them the same way — human, specific, not salesy. Do not A/B test subject lines in isolation. Test the entire email style." (Matt Lucero)
- "lowercase subject lines work better. 'hey {{firstname}}' and 'something for {{firstname}}' CONSISTENTLY outperform anything that sounds like it came from a marketing department" (7 principles)
- Diagnostics: "open rates tell you about targeting ... the subject line or the sender profile isn't resonating" (7 principles; see conflict C10: open tracking should be off).
- No other subject-line rules in the doc (length, punctuation, emoji): **UNKNOWN**.

### 1.8 Spintax
- "Spin Tax (Mandatory at Scale). Google and Microsoft fingerprint email copy ... if you send 2,000–10,000+ emails per day with identical or near-identical copy, they can detect the pattern and begin rejecting your emails." (Matt Lucero)
- Syntax: "{Hi|Hello|Hey} → one third of emails get 'Hi,' one third 'Hello,' one third 'Hey'". "Apply this across your entire email — subject line, opening, body, CTA. With 5–10 variation snippets, the multiplicative effect produces thousands of unique combinations."
- Evidence claim: "Matt ran a campaign sending 10,000+ emails/day without spin tax. Bounce rate skyrocketed to 12%".
- Side note: "sub-segment specificity adds a layer of syntax variation which additionally helps deliverability and engagement." (DiscoGen SOP)
- RULES wins: do not spin variables like {FIRST_NAME}; no spintax in client-facing text (see conflict C1).

### 1.9 Variables and personalisation
- "Never force AI to come up with a variable. The best variables are specific, naturally occurring sub-categories or sub-segments that AI can identify with high accuracy." / "Nick has never run a campaign where adding a specific sub-segment variable did not improve performance." (DiscoGen SOP, Use Case 1)
- "Provide a full sentence if using output as a campaign variable ... Otherwise the output won't fit naturally in context." (DiscoGen prompting best practices)
- Classification variables need definitions plus a fallback category (DiscoGen Use Case 2).
- Missing variable: "Do not remove those leads from the campaign. Use liquid syntax in your sequencer ... If case study variable does not exist → omit that sentence entirely" (Lead List SOP Part 3). RULES differs (conflict C2).
- Personalisation must be "specific and real": "You are calling out something specific and real about them." (Matt Part 1). Signals beat guesses: "If you know they use a specific EHR, specific software, are hiring for a specific role, or have a specific signal — you can write copy that calls that out directly".
- **Dulcy Signal rule:** "state one specific, verifiable observation about the company. Record the source URL, observation date, relevant date window and exact fact in the campaign evidence. Separate what was observed from what it might suggest; ads, hiring or a website gap do not prove poor performance."
- Strong personalisation examples: prospect's own phone number as merge field (branded calling); website keywords pulled by DiscoGen ("dental practice purchase, ..."); case study name ("PS love the work you did for [casestudycompanyname]").
- Segment-level: "Each segment = one campaign. You write specific copy for each segment. You do not mix segments." (Matt Lucero, Pillar 2). "Build a matrix of subtype x title. Write copy for every intersection. Match the angle to how your product impacts that specific role at that specific company type" (How to pull a lead list in 2026).
- Test: "can I write an email that feels like it was sent specifically to this person? If the list is too broad to do that, narrow it down." (Matt Lucero)

### 1.10 Offers (Scribe now owns offers: Forge folded in, 2026-11-24)
- "Even a perfectly written cold email cannot save a bad offer." (Matt Rule 5 caveat)
- "The strength of your offer determines outcome more than copy, channel, or creative." (Fazio core principle)
- "If you are selling a commodity service — and the vast majority of people are — your service alone will not convert on cold traffic." / "You must pitch an outcome, not a service." (Nate Behr)
- Exceptions where "we do X" works: "Signal-based campaigns" and "Right place, right time" (luck). (Nate Behr)
- "Service: what you do (mechanism). Offer: what they get + how fast + why it's safe (outcome + terms)" (Dulcy deck)
- "Your service is what you do. Your offer is why someone picks you over the 50 other companies in their inbox saying the same thing." (MCA dialogue)
- "Find the one thing that is actually different about your product or service and anchor your entire cold email copy to that." (branded calling TL;DR)
- "When the product is weak, lean into the lowest-friction entry point. Free and specific will always outperform vague and paid." (Slack ad bot)
- Frame as next step, not rebuild: "'Help me improve what's working' beats 'let me build this for you from scratch' almost every time." (Fazio #6)
- Name the burden removed: "name the specific operational pain you're removing ... rather than just naming the deliverable." (Fazio #7)
- Name the moat: "name the thing that makes you hard to replicate — certification, proprietary process, manufacturing relationship, exclusive data — even briefly." (Fazio #4)
- Don't underprice (Fazio #5, Dulcy guide #4). Never state a price in cold copy unless approved (CLAUDE.md #3).
- **Never invent the offer**: Nick's feedback example: "Remove the 'you don't pay unless they show' line — client can't offer that." Offers and guarantees come only from approved client fields (Cold Traffic Offer, Front End Offer(s), Outbound Risk Reversal).

### 1.11 Lead magnets / free work
- "Service Lead Magnet (Best Performing in 2026). Definition: A free deliverable that is directly tied to the paid outcome you sell." "This is not a digital lead magnet (ebook, guide, checklist)." (Nate Behr)
- Four requirements: "Fast to produce", "Repeatable and templatizable", "Automatable to some degree", "High perceived value". (Nate Behr; Dulcy deck: "Fast (minutes, not days) / Repeatable (template-able) / Automatable (AI/code) / High perceived value")
- "The test: read the cold email and ask yourself — why would my prospect say no to this? If you cannot think of a reason, it is a good offer."
- "The deliverable must be tangible. Audits and free consultations do not count — prospects know an audit ends with a pitch and a consultation is just a sales call rebranded."
- "Rule: relevance > free stuff ... Your free thing should preview the paid result" (Dulcy deck)
- Saturation: "The free lead list offer is now more saturated ... In saturated markets ... it is less effective but still outperforms a naked service pitch."
- Gift cards / AirPods: "only use this if you genuinely cannot think of a free deliverable tied to the paid outcome" (Nate Behr). Tier list: gift card "Not strong enough to be the offer, but very strong as a PS/add-on"; charity donation "rips", placed above gift cards. Any gift = spend/promise: client approval required.
- Lever priority: "Use these levers in priority order. If you can do Lever 1, do it. Add Lever 2 on top if possible. Fall back to Lever 3 only if 1 and 2 are not viable options." (Nate Behr)
- Intro offer: "Do not immediately sell the full service to a stranger. Extract one small result from the larger service" / "The intro offer should not be random free work." (Cold-Ready Offer SOP Steps 10-11)
- Free-offer appointment trade-off: "Lead magnet offers, Loom video audits, or any 'value first' style hooks → You will get more leads, but your appointment set rate will drop significantly — closer to 20%." (How Many Cold Emails SOP)
- **Dulcy asset honesty (Signal framework):** "Say 'we already have' or 'we mapped out' only when that exact asset exists and has been checked. Otherwise offer to create it, with approved scope, inputs and turnaround". "'No pitch attached' is usable only if the asset and delivery really contain no pitch."

### 1.12 Guarantees / risk reversal
- "A clear 'if we don't deliver X, you get Y' statement" tied "to the specific outcome the prospect wants". (Nate Behr)
- Structure: "If we do not achieve [specific measurable threshold] by [specific timeframe], then [specific remedy]." "The guarantee should reinforce the claim, not compensate for a weak offer." (Cold-Ready Offer SOP Step 9)
- Keep safe: "Only offer the guarantee to great-fit prospects", "Scope it to a milestone (week 1-2)", "Use 'work-for-free-until-X' or 'cancel-and-keep-deliverables' instead of unlimited refunds" (Dulcy deck)
- "A guarantee's job is to get the reply, not close the deal." (Dulcy guide #8)
- Ethics: "you should still state the terms clearly up front for ethical and retention reasons" (Fazio #11)
- Dulcy Signal framework: "Do not substitute an unsupported performance guarantee or hide a sales-call gate."
- Only use a guarantee the client has approved (Outbound Risk Reversal). Never invent one (CLAUDE.md #3).

### 1.13 Follow-ups
- "Length: 2–4 emails maximum. Beyond that, diminishing returns kick in and spam report rate increases." (Matt Lucero)
- "i run 3-5 follow ups MINIMUM on every campaign and each one reframes the offer slightly or adds a piece of value" (7 principles)
- Nick's copywriting skill "Outputs: a 2-step email sequence (Email 1 and Email 2)".
- Step 2/3 use: "worth flagging early in outreach sequencing (step 2/3 emails) as a reason 'once it's working, it just keeps working.'" (Fazio #10)
- Conflict: see C7. Engine default is 3 steps, 2 days apart (RULES 2026-11-18, 2026-11-23).

- The doc says nothing about signatures: **UNKNOWN in source**. RULES: no manual signature (2026-11-17).

### 1.15 Deliverability-driven copy rules
- "Never include in cold emails: Links — flags spam filters, higher perceived risk of virus/phishing. Images — same reason. HTML formatting — same reason ... Plain text only." (Matt Lucero, Deliverability Rules for Copy)
- "Turn on Plain Text Mode in your sending tool". "Disable open tracking. Open tracking works by embedding an invisible image pixel ... Turn it off."
- Spintax at scale (1.8). Spam words ("free" → "complimentary") (1.4).
- "When your email sounds like a broadcast, people report it as spam — which damages your infrastructure AND loses the reader." (Matt Lucero, 1-to-1 Rule)
- Follow-ups beyond 4: "spam report rate increases".
- List hygiene for copy: "Remove companies with email security (SEGs like Mimecast, Proofpoint) entirely if possible." (Alex's top notes)
- Infra notes (not copy, for context): run unbranded domains month one; "Group A inboxes get an extra two weeks of warmup. Group B gets 45-60 days before touching a live campaign." (Alex's top notes); minimum warm-up 14 days, ~20 emails/account/day, warm-up 20-30/day (Matt Lucero); ~15 emails/inbox/day (7 principles).
- "Changing the copy doesn't fix a sending problem" lives in the Campaign SOP (next-campaign-rules §2.6), not this doc; consistent with "Bad deliverability / weak infrastructure → still no results" (Nick, Claude Code SOP).
- RULES consistent: unsubscribe link off, opt-out via reply P.S. (2026-11-23).

### 1.16 Other rules
- "Rule 5: Pretend You Got This Email (The Most Important Rule)" ... "Click into individual LinkedIn profiles from your lead list. Become that person in your mind. Then read the email you're about to send them." (Matt)
- "Rule 3: Be Valuable to the Recipient." Ranked: "Actually give something for free inside the email — highest value; Offer to do something for free — second highest; Point out a problem they may not be aware of and offer a solution; Deliver an insight they probably weren't aware of". (Matt)
- "Multiple versions of the same email. Write at least 2 versions with different call out angles and different value prop framings." (Additional Notes)
- A/B: "Do not A/B test: Subject lines alone; Minor word changes or micro nuances. Do A/B test: Entire style of copy — e.g., direct response vs. curiosity-driven; Free deliverable vs. no free deliverable" (Matt Lucero). "I've NEVER seen tiny syntax changes consistently reproduce at our scale. When a test really moves, there's usually a bigger difference between the two versions (different angle, offer, proof, audience, etc)." (150M post)
- Don't try to close in email: "Cold email does not close deals. It gets replies." (Matt Lucero, Pillar 4)
- Channel fit: "Cold email works best for low-complexity, done-for-you, cheap-to-test offers." (Fazio #1)
- Human must review copy: "Claude cannot tell you if the CTA is weak or if the value prop is missing a key angle — you have to know that yourself" (Nick, Claude Code SOP)
- Input quality: "If your inputs are vague, your outputs will be generic regardless of how good your skills are built." (Nick)
- Feedback must be specific: "do not just say 'make it better.'" (Nick)
- "Signal framework ... is copy guidance only; it does not authorize campaign launch, sending, automation changes or new commercial terms."

- Is there a less saturated, higher-budget adjacent market for this same mechanism?
- Is my pain point specific enough that only the real ICP feels it — or is it generic enough to bore everyone?
- Is my proof a wall of recognizable names, or one isolated story?

### 3.6 Cold-Ready Offer: 5 rules, QA, escalation, Definition of Done (Dulcy SOP)
5 rules: "1. Make it stupid simple. 2. Speak to a problem or outcome specific to them. 3. Put it in their language. 4. Reverse risk with a specific guarantee. 5. Only require effort where it improves success."
QA ("Do not launch until all are true"):
- It is an offer, not a description of the service.
- The ICP is obvious.
- The outcome is specific.
- The result matters financially or operationally.
- The claim is believable.
- The offer contains worldview alignment.
- It uses language the ICP recognizes.
- The timeframe is clear.
- The mechanism explains why the result is possible.
- Prospect effort is minimal and purposeful.
- The guarantee is specific, if one is used.
- The intro offer proves competence.
- The intro offer naturally leads to the full service.
- A cold stranger could understand it without extra context.
Failure / escalation: generic → "Go deeper into the ICP and worldview alignment"; too good to be true → "Add or strengthen the mechanism"; big promise, weak responses → "Reduce the dream outcome to a smaller, more believable first result"; interest but no move → "Reduce time, effort, or perceived risk"; full offer too hard cold → "Create a smaller intro offer"; several plausible → "Test them."
Definition of Done: a cold prospect can quickly understand "1. Who this is for 2. What they get 3. Why it matters 4. Why they should believe it 5. Why it can happen quickly/easily 6. What they must do 7. What happens if the promised result is not delivered 8. What small first result they can experience before buying the full service".
Prerequisites before writing the offer: "exact ICP; specific role/persona; their desired business outcome; their current pain/problem; language they actually use; proof or experience relevant to them; mechanism that explains how the result is possible; specific piece of the full service that can be delivered as an intro offer".

### 3.7 Human-in-the-loop (Nick, Claude Code SOP)
"Choosing the offer ... Reviewing copy ... Approving list accuracy ... QA of the final output". Offer skill "Outputs: 3 offer options with a recommendation" and needs human review.

### 3.8 Variable / AI-output QA (DiscoGen and Lead List SOPs)

- "Case study names that may be inaccurate — spot check a few against the actual websites".

### 3.9 Discolike run checklist (list-side, for Scout)

### 5.1 Ratios and funnel (Nick, "How Many Cold Emails")
- Lead = "Anyone who shows interest at any level — 'Can I see pricing?', 'Can I get more info?', 'I'd be interested', 'Let's find a time to speak.'"
- Baseline: "Launch 3–5 campaigns / Send approximately 10,000–30,000 emails / ... Calculate your cumulative average across all of them — not just your best campaign".

### 6.4 DiscoGen prompting best practices (variables)
> Never force a variable. If you try to force AI to come up with something, error rate increases significantly and it will mess up frequently. Let the variable be something that flows naturally from what's already on the page.
> Give clear definitions for every classification. Don't assume AI knows what PLG means or what your ICP is. Define it explicitly in the prompt.
> Always provide a fallback option. Tell it what to output if none of your defined categories apply. Without this, it will force-fit everything and produce inaccurate results.
> Test on 5–10 records first. Before running against your full list, manually QA the output on a small sample to verify it's returning accurately. This prevents burning through a ton of credits on a bad prompt.
> Provide a full sentence if using output as a campaign variable. If the AI-generated output will be dropped directly into an email sentence, give it the full sentence in the prompt so it understands the expected format and word choice. Otherwise the output won't fit naturally in context.

Classification rule: "Define each category explicitly with a description — e.g., "PLG = [full description of what PLG is]", "Sales-Led = [full description]". Give it a fallback classification for records that don't fit either category — e.g., "If the company is an agency or not a software company, classify it as [X]""
ICP scoring: "Have it score each record on a scale of 0–10 — do NOT use yes/no".
Liquid: "Ask Claude or GPT to write the liquid syntax for you: paste the sequencer's liquid syntax documentation into GPT and ask it to write the if/then logic for your specific variable." (RULES uses fallback phrases instead; C2.)
ICP description: "Do not write 'we work with roofing contractors.' Use Claude or GPT to generate a detailed, thorough description of your ICP."
